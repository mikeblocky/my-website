To be honest, the quote, "You should also remember that your mother is a first-time mother too," has been echoing in my mind, particularly in relation to this chapter. I find it is a perfect summary of the core message here. I first encountered this idea when someone was discussing the flaws of parents, arguing that mothers and fathers are also navigating the difficult journey of parenthood for the very first time.

It is a compelling point. We often love our parents for the tangible gifts they have given us, such as the skills they taught us, the manners they instilled, or the guidance they offered. Yet, I believe this love does not automatically erase the pain they might have caused, whether that pain was emotional or physical. That "ugly" side does not simply vanish. It can leave permanent scars, shaping a child’s trauma, especially when a child is too young to understand why they might "deserve" such treatment.

The "selfishness" of first-time parents, as I see it, is a double-edged sword. They strive to give freedom and happiness to their child according to their own vision of what is best. But I have to wonder how often that vision truly aligns with what the child actually needs. I suspect it rarely aligns perfectly.

This chapter, for me, is fundamentally an experience. It plunges us into the perspective of Shima's mother on building a home and the profound struggles of being a parent. It serves as a sharp reminder that while parents are still learning, their actions and their very learning process can have lasting, sometimes devastating, impacts on their children.

Another thought that surfaced while I was processing this chapter is the powerful sentiment: "The children that you brought into this world never wanted to be here in the first place." Children do not ask to be born; their existence is a matter of fate or perhaps chance. Consequently, I feel it becomes the parents' deep responsibility to transform that initial lack of desire into a life their children can eventually love and embrace.

The relationship between a parent and child is undeniably complex. Each side carries expectations and burdens that can clash with the other person’s ideal of family. Children, at their core, long for happiness, love, and genuine care. Parents, in turn, often desire recognition and appreciation for the sacrifices they have made. Sometimes, these ideals do not mesh, creating a gap that makes honest conversation and mutual understanding incredibly difficult. This idea accidentally surfaced in this chapter: the mother’s vision of a mutually supportive family, constantly caring for each other, is not what Shima wants or perhaps even needs.

The dynamic between Shima and his mother, and even with her first husband, painfully shows how these conflicting ideals can break a family. His mother's desire to bring happiness, specifically by nurturing Shima’s acting talent, was not something they could achieve peacefully. It is so hard to piece things together when neither side seems willing to compromise or truly understand the other. Shima’s mother was undeniably harsh on him for pursuing acting, and her husband could not sustain the family under that immense strain. Day by day, those unspoken resentments and bottled-up feelings grew worse until everything inevitably shattered. We witnessed their relationship crumble, leaving behind a heavy weight and a house already broken.

In an attempt to fix this, Shima’s stepfather steps in, trying to hold together the fragile threads of their relationship. Later, the mother tries to mend things by introducing Shima's brother. But, as I see it, merely filling a gap does not count as a solution. Both sides must actively work to understand why things broke down in the first place. Holding a family together becomes immensely difficult when the love that once bound them has faded. It is not necessarily hate that replaces it, but a painful indifference, like a thick fog that will not clear. It is very hard to accept actions taken against us by someone we loved and trusted. The pain is not just that we were hurt; it is that the hurt was caused by someone we held dear.

The opening pages of this chapter offer a glimpse into the relationship between Shima’s stepfather and his mother. When the stepfather asked Shima if he remembered certain past conversations, the stepfather affirmed that he did, holding those words as a promise. He simply wants Shima to know that, regardless of the circumstances, he will be there for him as a listening ear and a source of support. He does not expect to be accepted as a father, but rather as a dependable adult Shima can lean on.

Mending a broken family is never simple. It requires time, immense effort, and many mistakes along the way. Within family relationships, this difficulty is even greater. We often resist even minor changes within our homes, such as a shift in attitude, different communication styles, or the introduction of new family members. These changes can feel threatening, shaking the very foundations of how we understand "family."

The line, "Because of you, I can give everything I have. Because I am your only existence here, I could do everything to protect you. I can sacrifice everything to let you shine. So please, my son, please save me too. Please let my life be worthy once again," is incredibly powerful. To me, this reveals the mother’s complicated desires. She seems to want to prove that with her alone, Shima could achieve more than what she and her first husband managed together. But it is also a painful confession of how her jealousy and her need to compete with that past family dynamic have damaged everyone. She wants Shima to validate her, to show her jealousy was somehow justified, and that she could "outdo" their past. It is a bittersweet plea. The latter part of her message, her realization that instead of helping her son, she caused emotional wounds, hits even harder. This, to me, is one of the biggest mistakes a parent can make: turning their child into a measure of their own worth. When that "proof" is missing, the parent’s identity and their role as a caregiver can fall apart.

The moment Shima’s mother confessed to him, mentioning her hospital diagnosis, was when I realized the damage was too deep for easy fixes. Shima had already built his defenses, a way to insulate himself from her compliments and a way to prevent anger from taking root because, in his mind, "it’s not worth it." No matter how hard she tries or how much she promises she has changed, it feels futile. Shima chose neglect as his coping mechanism, while his mother chose "fixing" as hers. But a pump full of holes, no matter how many patches are applied, will eventually leak. The holes remain, even if they are covered.

"And since then, he doesn’t cry or smile at me anymore." This observation makes the conversation between mother and child feel almost impossibly heavy. Even simple expressions, like encouragement, seem blocked by an invisible barrier. Over time, Shima’s mother has come to feel that she does not even know how to talk to him normally anymore. Single parenthood is inherently challenging, involving the burden of the past while trying to navigate the present. She has lived it, endured it, and grown tired of it. She does not want that weight to burden Shima again.

If I recall the previous chapters correctly, both the mother and Shima were trying to make the other person happy in their own ways. Yet, a sharp contrast emerged. His mother placed excessive pressure on him, imposing her definition of "best" while ignoring his attempts to communicate his feelings. Beneath her rhetoric lay her own fears and insecurities. Shima, on the other hand, was striving to make his mother proud, constantly pushing himself in his acting to meet her standards. The conversation between Taiga, Shima, and the mother reveals her deep misunderstanding. She thought his actions were about her, failing to see that her own actions were the true source of his pain. Shima even tried to protect her, asking Taiga not to speak to her so she wouldn't get hurt. He never told her about the gossip surrounding them, all to shield her. But she did not see it. She did not grasp that Shima had been protecting her all along. It is in these subtle details that the truth surfaces: we often ignore what is happening until the pain becomes undeniable.

But can we truly blame her? In some ways, perhaps not. The immense struggles of becoming a mother, then having to be a father as well, compounded by a lack of support, placed a huge burden on her. It is understandable that she felt overwhelmed. Yet, in other ways, I think she does bear responsibility. She did not strive to do better in the most important area: connection. She never seemed to step outside her protective shell to truly communicate with him, face to face and heart to heart.

However, one thing I admire about her character is her eventual admission that her flaws were hurting him. As I noted earlier, being a first-time parent is hard. For me, the goal isn't to be a "perfect" parent, but a "good" parent: one who can see their flaws and commit to changing them. Her journey speaks to the experience of many mothers who, in their blind insistence that they know the right way to raise a child, accidentally cause hurt. The hope for characters like her lies in their realization that they have made a mistake. They then strive to understand, driven by a genuine desire for their child’s happiness, ultimately placing that well-being above their own goals.

It is a common human trait that we are not always comfortable with compliments, especially from those closest to us. We carry our ego like a shield against the vulnerability that praise can bring. Parents are also often afraid of making mistakes or even of being praised by their children because they are aware that any flaw might ruin the perfect image their child has of them. But here lies the contradiction: parents may think this way, yet the child often already sees the flaws. It is this misplaced confidence in that "protective shield" that ultimately hurts everyone. It is not just the children who suffer; others, like Ririka, who had no direct relationship with the mother, also suffered.

The most traumatic scars often begin in childhood. Damage tends to create more damage. Even though Shima’s mother eventually realized her mistakes and wanted to fix them, I feel it was too late because the pain had already sunk deep into Shima’s heart. It is like trying to put an onion back together after peeling it layer by layer; it simply cannot be done. Healing takes time and immense effort.

The most painful part is the creation of a "monster" of defense within Shima. And the truly tragic part? His mother herself was its creator. This brings me back to the parallels with Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. The relationship between Victor Frankenstein and his creature mirrors the fragile, painful bond between Shima and his mother. Victor, driven by ambition, brought his creature to life, intending it to be extraordinary. Yet, the moment the creature opened its eyes, Victor felt only fear and disgust. He rejected his own creation, abandoning it without guidance or love. The relationship between Shima and his mother carries a similar weight.

Like Victor, the mother's desire to mold her son into something that would validate her own worth led to profound harm. She hoped to shape him into a star who would reflect her sacrifices to the world. But in doing so, she overlooked his humanity, his needs, and his own voice. The creature's agony came not from its form, but from its creator's rejection. That rejection taught the creature it was unworthy of love. Shima, too, learned to shield himself from pain by suppressing his emotions, his tears, and his smiles, against the person who first made him feel unworthy.

Both Victor and Shima's mother ultimately faced the consequences of their creations. The creature’s rage became a testament to Victor's failure as a "parent," just as Shima’s silent defenses became a mirror for his mother, reflecting the wounds she had caused. The monsters in both stories were not born but made, layer by layer and moment by moment. In both tales, the creators' attempts to fix the damage came too late because what had been broken had already learned to defend itself.

However, change takes time, and now this "monster" within Shima is starting to realize he does not have to be alone anymore. This is where Shima's path is different from the Frankenstein Creature. While the Creature stayed isolated and met only with horror, Shima finds the courage to confront what happened to him. He begins to face it not with hatred or a desire for revenge, but with growing empathy. The love from those around him, such as the knowledge that people will support him unconditionally, gives him the power to take a step forward. Even his stepfather, who asks for nothing in return, helps him find this strength.

Unlike the Creature, whose goodness was crushed by abandonment, Shima can start to move at the same pace as those around him. He gains the strength to look back at his past and accept his vulnerability. It is only when he truly realizes he is loved that he can revisit the past, let it go with peace, and understand that he is not solely to blame. He no longer needs to see himself as the villain of his own story. Shima just needs to be himself and open up more.

Through his experiences in high school, he has begun to find himself and reclaim his "humanity." This is the key difference; while the Creature's story is about humanity lost, Shima's is about humanity rediscovered. He now wants to be someone trustworthy and a better person. His beauty lies in this rediscovered self. Reclaiming his humanity shows his desire to forgive himself and to be forgiven for his mistakes. This mutual forgiveness was impossible for Victor and his Creature. Inviting his mom to this year’s festival is a huge step in mending their relationship, an act of hope that is missing from the tragedy of Frankenstein.

As the curtain rises, I hope this play marks a turning point that allows Shima to speak with his mother again. An embrace between them could mean they are finally accepting and forgiving the painful memories they both carry, turning what was "forgettable" into a shared path toward healing.

Ultimately, it is vital to remember that while it may be a mother's first time navigating the maze of parenthood, the words spoken and the wounds caused still stay with the child. Trauma is often not born from the event itself, but from the lack of support and understanding at that critical moment. Furthermore, there is an imbalance in expecting a child to fully empathize with the complex life of an adult. The adult, who was once a child, has a broader perspective and therefore a greater responsibility to bridge that gap. This consideration keeps the child's experience at the center, even as we acknowledge the parent's own journey of learning and growth.
