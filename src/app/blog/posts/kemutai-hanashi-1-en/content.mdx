# Chapter 1 – Where there is smoke, there is fire

*You can read the Japanese version of this [here](/blog/kemutai-hanashi-1-jp).*

*To truly savor this story, please consider supporting the author by reading the latest chapters on [COMIC【nettai】](https://www.comicnettai.com/book/9) and purchasing the volumes on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.co.jp/stores/%E6%9E%97-%E5%8F%B2%E4%B9%9F/author/B09YTVZTTR?language=en&ref=ap_rdr&isDramIntegrated=true&shoppingPortalEnabled=true).*

I have never invested so much of myself in a manga before. Most I’ve encountered were fleeting experiences, leaving little trace in my memory. But this manga, *Kemutai Hanashi*, unveiled something different. Reading it felt like stepping into a familiar, yet undefined space that has always lingered in the quiet corners of my mind—a space dedicated to the perennial quest of understanding where we are, and who we are, in this world.

I discovered it in the liminal space after graduating high school, during that uncertain interlude before university life began. I was navigating the contours of my own self, my worth, and my direction, and this manga, in some uncanny way, mirrored that journey. Some chapters resonated more profoundly than others, yet all of them drew me closer to understanding parts of myself I had long forgotten.

The first three pages alone created a sensation that remains difficult to describe.

> “Where there is smoke, there is fire.”
>
> “Only when we notice the smoke do we start to wonder where it comes from.”

Since childhood, we are taught that smoke signifies fire. But have we ever truly paused to question this assertion? Do we simply assume, “Ah, there is fire,” without inquiring into its nature, or the genesis of its spark?

Life unfurls so swiftly that we seldom pause to observe the finer details. We might presume the smoke emanates from a building or a house, a place familiar. Yet, is that the entirety of it? True curiosity doesn't rest at surface-level answers. It is only when something touches us intimately that we begin to ask ourselves *why*.

It reminds me of an incident in my town a few weeks ago: I saw thick, black smoke unfurling against the sky and couldn't help but wonder about its origin. It is these small things, subtle yet arresting, that grant us pause. Only when they strike a chord within us do we begin to question. We start to wonder not just *where*, but *how*. How did it ignite? Why does it feel as though I cannot look away?

We become vulnerable when we delve into the recesses of ourselves. We hesitate to return to who we once were, because we have glimpsed a part of our being we cannot unknow. We may look away, afraid of what we might find, yet we cannot turn back either. We yearn to understand why we are the way we are. Why we are here.

From this point, the story commences. Takeda and Arita meet to move Takeda’s belongings into Arita’s home, and their interaction is one of the most genuinely heartwarming exchanges I’ve witnessed in a manga.

The way they converse on SNS eloquently reveals their personalities. Though this is only the beginning, I sense that Takeda possesses a warm, gentle heart. The way he used the “Thanks” emoji was a small but endearing detail that brought a smile to my face, suggesting a person who naturally expresses care in even the most minute gestures.

Arita, conversely, is more direct. His messages are brief, but this doesn't render him impolite. He seems to be the type who demonstrates care through action rather than words. For instance, though his messages are short, he still ensures Takeda knows he has arrived—a subtle but clear effort.

Later in the chapter, we see more small details that reveal their dimensions. Arita asks numerous questions: how things are progressing, whether everything will fit in the car, if Takeda needs help with the boxes. These small actions speak volumes about his personality. He clearly cares, even if he doesn't voice it explicitly.

A wonderful contrast between them emerges as they carry the boxes of books. Takeda appears optimistic, while Arita is utterly weary, wanting to just discard the heavy boxes. The difference in their expressions infuses the scene with a gentle humor; it’s a moment that feels both significant and endearingly absurd. This dynamic often occurs in close relationships—the desire to help a friend warring with the sheer effort of the task. It’s what makes the scene so amusing and achingly familiar. Despite the exhaustion, they continue, carrying the boxes together, one step at a time.

During the move, we encounter a truly heartwarming moment: Takeda "saving the cat." It’s a small, perhaps easily missed detail, but it speaks volumes. The way he calls out, “Hey there!” a bit too loudly, surprising the cat, is comical but also reveals something tender about him. He notices the little things. Even if his approach isn't perfect, his instinct is to help. That kind of intention is precious.

Inside the car, another charming contrast unfolds. Arita looks composed behind the wheel, his face serious, while Takeda attempts to mirror his expression, but it’s endearingly askew. Then comes the joke: a 10,000 yen discount for complimenting Arita. It’s the kind of jest that only works between people who share a long and comfortable history.

This kind of easy back-and-forth is something to be treasured. It reflects a friendship where you can speak without the weight of deliberation, a connection that feels light on the surface but holds a quiet depth.

While carrying boxes into the new house, Takeda makes a point to thank the person who lent them the car. He hadn't forgotten. This speaks to his character: polite, thoughtful, and always endeavoring to be kind. He doesn’t overlook anyone, which is truly admirable.

But it also led me to contemplate the potential burden of being "the kind one." When generosity is a constant, it can sometimes become an unseen weight. People can grow accustomed to it, not realizing the effort being expended because they are used to you always giving. There’s a quiet vulnerability in offering so much of oneself, a risk of feeling that your kindness is consumed rather than truly seen.

Then comes a moment of pure, delightful comedy: Takeda and Arita realize they have both brought a toaster, completely forgetting who was meant to bring the microwave. A mix-up like this is so true to life—plans that sound straightforward descending into a gentle chaos. It’s relatable and genuinely funny, one of those unassuming, tender moments that often become the most deeply etched in our memories. Of course, Takeda, ever the optimist, sees the bright side: now they can toast four slices of bread at once!

Reading this chapter stirred a cascade of my own recollections—the kind that seem insignificant in the moment but return later with unexpected gravity. It brought to mind a school field trip, the only one I ever attended. Now, whenever something reminds me of it, I find my eyes welling, perhaps because it was a precious interlude of genuine camaraderie with my classmates, a simple yet indelible memory.

The way smoke is depicted in the manga now resonates with a newfound clarity. When it drifts in, it commands the entire vista; suddenly, it’s just you and the smoke, alone together. The moment is imbued with a delicate sensitivity, capturing your entire focus as everything else recedes into quietude.

Later, as Takeda brings boxes downstairs, he overhears a child and their mother, a simple exchange about not running on the stairs and what's for lunch. While I've never been drawn to apartment living, I appreciate moments like that—hearing people out there simply… living. Their everyday routines are comforting. Silence can be peaceful, but an excess of it can feel profoundly isolating. The scene was suffused with warmth.

As someone not from Japan, the custom of *hikkoshi soba* (soba given to new neighbors) was an entirely new discovery. Learning about these facets of a culture is a joy. When Arita asked Takeda about the cultural meaning of soba, I was compelled to research it myself. As Takeda explained, soba represents longevity, health, and prosperity; some believe it even protects against bad luck. There is a subtle artistry in how soba is made and served, and it feels like so many Japanese dishes carry an intrinsic beauty and thoughtful intention.

The scene where Arita’s parents send him an abundance of soba packages is also touching. It’s a familiar expression of parental love—showering their children with provisions, their deepest wish always being for their well-being.

The conversation that flows between them while cooking is another remarkable detail. So often, cooking is depicted as a solitary act, but here, the dialogue seems to season the food itself. And the result? Arita serving the zarusoba on a colander is one of the most memorable and hilarious sights. It brings to mind the minor disasters of one's own first attempts at cooking—a humbling, yet fond memory.

As the chapter closes, Takeda stands on the balcony, observing the town at night. I do the same whenever I move to a new place—gazing at the buildings, the sky, the neighboring houses. As Takeda says, “things at night are so different from daytime, you can look at the lit-up windows and wonder what kind of life is behind them.” Light is a fount of energy, a beacon of warmth, and the very pulse of a home. You cannot see fire without the light it casts, nor feel warmth without the light that shines. Light is simple, yet it is where a story begins.

It is a gentle closure that heralds a new opening. The light, the fire, has now been kindled. What is its color, and what will it consume? That is the enigma that awaits. An idiom from my country says, “Small rain lays great dust,” suggesting that with patience and persistence, one can achieve their desire. We cannot take shortcuts to find an answer. Fire does not start, spread, or become noticed without reason. Everything unfolds for a reason. It is a narrative veiled in mist, awaiting discovery.

This first chapter stirred a multitude of deep feelings and memories. Within its pages, you might find yourself; I certainly recognized fragments of my own life there. I love everything about it: the construction, the dynamics between Arita and Takeda, and the unvarnished yet beautiful articulation of thought. This chapter resonated deeply, and I look forward to exploring the next.
